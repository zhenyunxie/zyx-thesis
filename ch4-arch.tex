% Motivation of this chapter
In the previous chapter, optical networking functionalities were demonstrated on a general-purpose photonic processor based on a hexagonal mesh topology.
By properly configuring the PUCs, various optical circuits, including optical interconnects, multicasting, and circuit switching, were implemented within a single reconfigurable platform.
However, the scalability of this approach becomes limited when implementing large-scale optical circuit switches.
As the switch radix increases, the number of required programmable elements grows significantly, leading to increased hardware complexity, accumulated optical loss, and higher control overhead.
These factors make it challenging to efficiently realize high-radix switching fabrics using a fully general purpose photonic mesh architecture.

% Objective of this chapter
In this chapter, we propose the use of graphs to model OCS in silicon photonics, enabling routing analysis, blocking simulation, hardware complexity extraction and optical performance.
We analyze and benchmark multiple architectures in terms of optical loss, signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), and hardware complexity, comparing them with state-of-the-art silicon photonics integration capabilities to extrapolate feasible scenarios in the near to mid-term \cite{perez2025large, giewont300mmMonolithicSilicon2019}.
The overall goal of this chapter is to provide a comprehensive benchmark among alternative circuit architectures for high-radix silicon photonics OCS that meet the performance requirements of modern networking applications.
\section{Graph-based modelling of integrated photonic switches}\label{sec:graph_modelling} % (fold)
Integrated photonic switches consist of arrangements of basic tunable switching cells, where an input array of signals can be spatially or spectrally routed to an array of optical outputs.
To study their properties, integrated photonic circuits can be modeled based on the graph layer that presented previous chapter~\ref{chap:fundamentals}.
This mathematical representation facilitates the analysis of switching networks and their blocking probabilities by applying pathfinding algorithms directly to the graph.
The performance of the switch strongly depends on its architecture, which ultimately dictates the number of switching and crossing cells, stages to go through, crosstalk and blocking type.

Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-archs} depicts the different network architectures under study, which are commonly used in photonic switching \cite{chengSiliconPhotonicSwitch2020}.
The Benes network requires the lowest number of switch cells and stages \cite{benesMathematicalTheoryConnecting1965a}; however, it exhibits first-order crosstalk and is rearrangeable non-blocking (RNB), meaning that accommodating a new port request may require rearranging existing connections.
The Dilated Benes network introduces additional switching cells to ensure that only a single signal traverses each node \cite{1096722}, resulting in second-order crosstalk, while remaining RNB.
In this study, we also consider a vertically stacked network \cite{leaMultiLogNetworksTheir, shyyLogSub21991} composed of two Dilated Benes fabrics, forming a Doubled Dilated Benes architecture.
The motivation is to investigate the impact on performance and blocking probability of adding a second fabric in parallel.
For the sake of comparison, two additional networks are also considered.
The PILOSS network \cite{suzukiUltracompact882014}, derived from the crossbar, provides balanced loss across all possible paths and is wide-sense non-blocking (WSNB), meaning that any new connection can be established provided the network is routed using an appropriate algorithm.
The final network considered is the Dilated Banyan \cite{aliPerformanceEnhancement8times82021}, which completely suppresses crosstalk and is strictly non-blocking (SNB); this network does not require any specific routing algorithm, as there exists a single disjoint path between each input and output port.
\subsection{Network conflicts} \label{sub:network_conflicts} % (fold)
Network conflicts represent a key specification in switching. It defines how the switch behaves for routing requests that could potentially impact the routing operation of the other channels.
We define two types of conflicts that can occur in the network.
The first captures the blocking that arises when an input port cannot be connected to an output port due to the absence of available paths.
In this study, rearrangement is not allowed, so even RNB networks may experience blocking.
This is because interrupting already established connections can significantly impact the network performance, in applications such as data centers running AI workloads, flows are often long-lived and high-bandwidth, making the rearrangement of existing connections inefficient \cite{wuACTINAAdaptingCircuitSwitching2025}.
Nevertheless, for scheduled traffic \cite{wangTopoopTCooptimizingNetwork}, low blocking networks might still find applications and that is the reason why they are included in the study.
The second type of conflict occurs when a single node in the network is shared by two signals, resulting in first-order crosstalk.
In silicon photonics, 2$\times$2 MZI leakage to the undesired output port is strongly wavelength-dependent and can be relatively high, which affects the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and overall link performance.

Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-routing} illustrates how these conflicts can arise in an 8$\times$8 Dilated Benes network.
In this example, input port 5 is intended to connect to output port 7, with up to four paths available to establish the interconnect (Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-routing}(a)).
After selecting one path, a new port request—from input 7 to output 5—arises.
A conflict occurs between the two paths shown in blue and yellow in Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-routing}(b), as both attempt to exit through the same output and share the same edge, marked with a red cross.
This is physically impossible and is classified as conflict of type 1, due to blocking.
The second type of conflict is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-routing}(c), where another path in yellow—from input 7 to output 3—shares a node marked with a red cross.
While two signals can cross in a 2$\times$2 MZI in a photonic circuit, this generates first-order crosstalk, identified as conflict of type 2.

\subsection{Routing algorithms} \label{sub:routing_algorithms} % (fold)
As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-routing}, the RNB architecture provides multiple connectivity paths between an input-output (IO) pair; and thus, the routing algorithm plays a crucial role in determining the blocking rate.
In this study, simulations are based on a sequential routing scheme, where connection requests are provisioned serially without knowledge of future demands.
Consequently, suboptimal path selection for an early request may block subsequent connections, making the path selection strategy critical.

In this work, we investigated two routing strategies: a random strategy, which selects a path randomly from the pool of available candidates, and a sorted strategy, also known as the minimum index (MI) \cite{changWidesenseNonblockingMulti2006, guoWidesenseNonblockingNew2013}, which systematically prioritizes the uppermost available path within the topology—ranging from Path 1 to Path 4 in Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-routing}(a).
These two algorithms generate different routing patterns within the network when the switch is fully occupied.
The random strategy distributes paths uniformly across the network, whereas the sorted strategy populates the upper paths first, leaving the lower paths idle.

It is also worth noting that, beyond sequential routing, the full set of IO pairs can be configured simultaneously in RNB networks using synchronous switching \cite{chakrabartyMatrixBasedNonblockingRouting2009, karimiIntroductionAnalysisOptimal2014, zulfinImplementationRoutingDivision2020}, allowing global optimization of the routing and the establishment of non-blocking connections for the requested IO pairs.
However, in this study we focus on asynchronous switching, reflecting the non-fully scheduled traffic of real-world use cases, which generates unpredictable IO requests over time.
Such asynchronous traffic models have also been adopted in prior study to evaluate the worst-case routing behavior and blocking characteristics of optical switching architecture \cite{8535157}.
Under asynchronous switching, different network architectures exhibit distinct blocking characteristics.

WSNB and SNB topologies, such as the PILOSS and Dilated Banyan networks, guarantee that any idle IO pair can be connected without blocking or the need to rearrange established paths.
In contrast, RNB topologies, such as the Benes and Dilated Benes networks, may experience blocking, as they provide multiple shared paths for each IO pair, which can lead to both types of conflict.
\section{Hardware complexity}\label{sec:hardware_complexity} % (fold)
Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-hw-complexity} shows the hardware utilization of the networks considered in this study, in terms of the number of switching cells, stages, and crossings.
The number of switching cells are associated to complexity in packaging and driving operations, while the number of stages and crossings are associated to signal integrity and loss.
For Benes-like networks, scalability grows on the order of $\mathcal{O}(N \log N)$, the number of cells approximately doubles when the number of ports is doubled, whereas for the PILOSS and Dilated Banyan networks, scale as $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$, the number of switching cells increases by a factor of four when the network size doubles.
As previously noted, the Benes network has the lowest number of switching cells, since all paths are occupied when the switch is fully loaded.
Beyond this, the other networks require an increasing number of switching cells, with the Dilated Banyan network having the highest count—over 32,000 cells for a 128$\times$128 configuration (Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-hw-complexity}(a)).

In terms of stages defined as the number of switching cells a signal traverses from an input to an output port, Benes-like and Dilated Banyan networks scale logarithmically with the number of ports, whereas the PILOSS network scales linearly, with a 1:1 ratio.
This represents a significant difference compared to the other network types: for a 128$\times$128 switch, the PILOSS network traverses 128 switching cells, whereas Benes-like networks require only 12-16 stages.

Conversely, PILOSS is the network with the fewest crossings that a signal traverses from an input to an output port, and this value is the same for any IO pair.
In contrast, Benes-like and Dilated Banyan networks exhibit a roughly normal distribution of crossings (from 0 to hundreds) per path connecting an IO pair, resulting in imbalanced behavior.
The 128$\times$128 Double Dilated Benes network has the highest number of crossings—almost 700 in the worst-case path (from input 1 to output 128)—with a mean of approximately 360 crossings.

\section{Optical performance}\label{sec:optical_performance} % (fold)
For the optical performance shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-optical-performance}, we simulated the photonic circuits based on the network graphs, incorporating the S-parameters of the switching cells and crossings at each node.
The transfer-matrix method \cite{baliliTRANSFERMATRIXMETHOD2012} was applied to the complete switch matrices to simulate crosstalk at the undesired ports.
The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) refers to the attenuation-to-crosstalk ratio (ACR), defined at each output port as the ratio between the received power of the intended signal and the total accumulated crosstalk power. For output port $i$, the SNR is given by
\begin{equation}
    SNR_i = \frac{P_{s,i}}{\sum_k P_{xt,i-k}},
\end{equation}
where $P_{s,i}$ denotes the received optical power at output port $i$, including all propagation and insertion losses along the selected routing path, and $P_{xt,i-k}$ represents the power leakage from undesired input channels contributing to crosstalk at the same output port.
The SNR was computed as a function of wavelength, with the leakage of the switching and crossing cells derived from typical extinction ratio (ER) values, using standard silicon photonic components and thermo-optic phase shifting \cite{gomez-hidalgoFundamentalBuildingBlocksa} (see Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-optical-performance}(a)).

The crosstalk in the switching cells is strongly dependent on the spectral response in the O-band region, reaching approximately –40 dB at center wavelengths and –20 dB at corner wavelengths.
This occurs due to the thermo-optic phase shifter response and the uneven splitting ratio of the 2$\times$2 MZI considered in this study.
The inherent wavelength-dependent phase shift produces the exact phase for maximum extinction ratio (ER) at a given central point in the spectrum, but the ER decreases outside this wavelength due to phase tuning inaccuracies, with the added effect of the uneven splitting ratio.
In contrast, the ER for the crossings remains below –60 dB and flat across the entire O-band, if properly designed.

Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-optical-performance}(b) shows the SNR for the different architectures and radices, up to 64 ports; results for 128 ports could not be obtained due to the large computational resources required to calculate the transfer matrix for such a large number of elements and radix.
To enable consistent SNR comparison, all networks were configured following an identity port mapping (\textit{$\text{In}_1\text{--}\text{Out}_1, \text{In}_2\text{--}\text{Out}_2, \dots, \text{In}_N\text{--}\text{Out}_N$}), using the sorted routing strategy to establish the network paths.
The Benes network exhibits the worst SNR for low-radix switches (below 32 ports), reaching values around 10 dB at corner wavelengths.
The Dilated Banyan network shows the best SNR, above 40 dB across the entire O-band, including for high-radix switches of 64 ports.
Dilated networks present relatively good SNR values, ranging between 20 and 60 dB depending on the wavelength.
In contrast, the PILOSS SNR deteriorates significantly for high radix switches due to the large number of stages and the resulting increase in crosstalk sources, which lowers the overall SNR.

For the loss simulations, we considered the values reported in Table~\ref{tab:ch4-sota-bb} for state-of-the-art building blocks.
In addition, to calculate the overall photonic integrated circuit (PIC) loss, we included other components such as edge couplers, polarization elements, and propagation loss.
In this case, computing the full matrix of the entire architecture was not necessary; instead, losses were calculated along individual paths by summing the contributions from switching and crossing elements together with the waveguide propagation loss.
The propagation loss was estimated by assuming a uniform waveguide length of 5 mm for all routing paths, corresponding to approximately one quarter of a typical single reticle around 2-3 cm~\cite{meiling2008performance}, longer waveguides can also be considered with lower propagation loss below 0.1dB/cm~\cite{wgpropagationloss}.
The results are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-optical-performance}(c) for all networks considered in this study.
Boxplots illustrate the probability distribution of the loss, reflecting the imbalance among IO pairs due to variations in the number of crossings.
The Benes network exhibits the lowest loss, owing to its reduced number of stages and crossings.
The Dilated Banyan network also maintains very low loss, with values below 6 dB for a 128$\times$128 radix and a variability of approximately 1 dB between the lowest- and highest-loss paths.
Dilated architectures show losses below 7 dB with variability under 2 dB for the 128$\times$128 radix.
In contrast, the PILOSS network experiences a dramatic increase in loss for high-radix switches—above 25 dB for the 128$\times$128—, primarily due to the large number of stages, which is the main source of loss and limits scalability.
Only the 32x32 configuration remains with relatively good values below 10 dB.

\section{Blocking probability}\label{sec:blocking_probability} % (fold)
Two classical probabilistic models have been proposed by Lee~\cite{leeblocking} and Jacobaeus~\cite{lutton1983blocking} to analyze the blocking behavior of Clos networks in electronic switching fabrics.
Similar concepts have subsequently been extended to photonic switch to evaluate how topology affects path contention and scalability~\cite{fukushimaRoutingAlgorithmsPacket2008a,xiaohongjiangPerformanceModelingAlloptical2005}.
In this section we we adapt and replicate these methodologies to simulate the blocking rate of the given architectures under asynchronous switching, i.e., receiving sequential one-by-one port requests.
We designed two different experiments: the first, referred to as the incremental experiment, evaluates blocking as the switch is loaded from 0 to 1, representing 0\% to 100\% utilization of the switch radix.
The second, referred to as the dynamic experiment, assigns a lifetime to each port request and includes both the arrival and departure of connections.

\subsection{Incremental experiment}\label{subsection:incremental_experiment}
The pseudocode of the incremental experiment is shown in Algorithm~\ref{alg:incremental_experiment}.
First, the algorithm computes the switch state matrix, which defines the set of all possible paths between all possible input-output pairs (line 1).
During each iteration, the set of established paths $P_{\text{est}}$ is initialized (line 3) and a set of port requests $R$ is generated according to the load, each request $r \in R$ corresponds to a single connection request with a specific I/O port pair.
For each request, the algorithm retrieves all candidate paths and invokes the \textit{RearrangePaths} function to prioritize them according to the selected routing strategy.
This step constitutes the core of the algorithm: the strategy can either select the uppermost path in the topology (referred to sorted) or choose a path randomly (referred to random).
During the collision-checking phase (lines 9-15), the algorithm sequentially traverses the reordered paths and uses the \textit{CheckConflict} function to determine whether the current path conflicts with any path in $P_{\text{est}}$.
Once a conflict-free path is found, it is immediately assigned to the current request, and the inner loop is terminated.
If no available path is found after all candidate paths have been evaluated, the current iteration is marked as blocked, and the algorithm proceeds to the next iteration.
Consequently, the blocking rate per iteration (PI) is computed as the ratio of the number of blocked iterations to the total number of iterations:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:br_pi}
    \text{BR}_{\text{per iteration}} = \frac{\text{blocked iterations}}{\text{total iterations}}
\end{equation}
% Incremental algorithm
\begin{algorithm}[h]
    \caption{Incremental experiment of blocking rate}
    \label{alg:incremental_experiment}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \Require $G$ (Switch graph topology), $Type$ (strategy), $Load$ (Int), $TrafficIterations$ (Int)

        \State $M \gets \Call{GetSwitchMatrix}{G}$

        \For{each $iteration$ in $TrafficIterations$}
        \State $P_{est} \gets \emptyset$
        \State $R \gets \Call{RqstGenerator}{Load}$

        \For{each request pair $r$ in $R$}
        \State $blocking \gets \text{true}$
        \State $Paths \gets M[r]$
        \State $SortedPaths \gets \Call{RearrangePaths}{Paths, Type}$

        \For{each path $p$ in $SortedPaths$}
        \If{$\Call{CheckConflict}{p, P_{est}}$ is \textbf{false}}
        \State $P_{est}[r] \gets p$
        \State $blocking \gets \text{false}$
        \State \textbf{break}
        \EndIf
        \EndFor

        \If{$blocking$ is \textbf{true}}
        \State \textbf{Mark} $iteration$ as blocked
        \State \textbf{break}
        \EndIf
        \EndFor
        \EndFor
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-incremental-exp} shows the simulation results for the incremental experiments.
A total of $10^7$ iterations were simulated to evaluate the blocking rate as a function of switch load.
The upper panels (Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-incremental-exp}(a)) depict the blocking rate for the random routing strategy, while the lower panels (Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-incremental-exp}(b)) correspond to the sorted routing strategy.
The sorted algorithm yields lower blocking rates than the random strategy, as it tends to concentrate connections, effectively reserving free regions of the switch for future port requests and highlighting the importance of intelligent routing algorithms for hardware scalability.
Both conflict types are shown, with conflict of type 2 being more restrictive, as it does not allow switching cell sharing between signals and therefore results in higher blocking rates than conflict of type 1.

        Blocking rate as a function of switch load under the random and sorted routing strategy, (a) and (b), respectively.

Across all architectures, blocking increases with both radix and load, since a larger number of established paths increases the likelihood of conflicts.
The standard Benes architecture exhibits high blocking probabilities in all scenarios, even under low load conditions ($<0.1$).
As the radix increases to 128, the blocking probability rapidly approaches unity, confirming that, without dilation, this architecture is not suitable for blocking-sensitive applications.
Introducing dilation significantly reduces the blocking rate.
The Dilated Benes architecture substantially lowers blocking for conflict of type 1 across all radices.
However, under the stricter conflict of type 2 constraint, its limitations become evident, particularly at large radix (N = 128), where the blocking threshold is markedly reduced compared to the conflict of type 1 case.
Notably, the Double Dilated Benes architecture exhibits zero blocking for conflict of type 1 and shows low blocking values under conflict of type 2.
Even at high load, the Double Dilated Benes network, under the sorted strategy and the more restrictive conflict model, maintains a blocking rate below $10^{-1}$ for all radices, significantly outperforming the other Benes-based architectures.

\subsection{Dynamic experiment}\label{subsection:dynamic_experiment}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:dynamic_experiment} outlines the dynamic experiment, which calculates blocking rates by assigning a finite lifetime to each port request and subsequently releasing the associated connections in the switch.
The lifetime of each input-output request is modeled by the following exponential distribution:
\begin{equation}
    f(t) = \mu e^{-\mu t}, \quad t \ge 0
    \label{eq:lifetime}
\end{equation}
where $\mu$ is the life-time parameter, and its inverse determines the mean duration of each connection.
This distribution is memory-less and is consistent with Poisson traffic in classical switching theory.
For example, $\mu = 0.1$ corresponds to a connection that lasts, on average, 10 iterations.
The request intensity is managed by the arrival rate $\lambda$, defined as the probability of a request arriving at each available port.
This parameter controls the input load by determining how many available ports will generate new requests.
For instance, an arrival rate of 0.5 implies that each idle port has a 50\% probability of receiving a request in an iteration.
During each iteration, the request generator produces a set of port requests $R$ based on the available ports, the arrival rate $\lambda$ and the lifetime parameter $\mu$.
The subsequent procedure follows the incremental experiment, where a conflict-free interconnect path is selected according to the defined routing strategy.
If no valid path is found, the request is marked as blocked, and the blocking rate per request (PR) is computed:
\begin{equation}
    \text{BR}_{\text{per request}} = \frac{\text{blocked requests}}{\text{total requests}}
    \label{eq:br_pr}
\end{equation}
% Dynamic algorithm
\begin{algorithm}[h]
    \caption{Dynamic experiment of blocking rate}
    \label{alg:dynamic_experiment}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \Require $G$ (Switch graph topology), $Type$ (strategy), $\mu$ (holding time), $\lambda$ (arrival rate), $TrafficIterations$ (Int)

        \State $M \gets \Call{GetSwitchMatrix}{G}$
        \State $P_{est} \gets \emptyset$

        \For{each $iteration$ in $TrafficIterations$}
        \State $AvailablePorts \gets \Call{GetAvailabePorts}{}$
        \State $R \gets \Call{RqstGenerator}{AvailablePorts, \mu, \lambda}$

        \For{each request pair $r$ in $R$}
        \State $blocking \gets \text{True}$
        \State $Paths \gets M[r]$
        \State $SortedPaths \gets \Call{RearrangePaths}{Paths, Type}$

        \For{each path $p$ in $SortedPaths$}
        \If{$\Call{CheckConflict}{p, P_{est}}$ is \textbf{false}}
        \State $P_{est}[r] \gets p$
        \State $blocking \gets \text{False}$
        \State \textbf{break}
        \EndIf
        \EndFor

        \If{$blocking$ is \textbf{True}}
        \State \textbf{Mark} request $r$ as blocked
        \State \textbf{Mark} $iteration$ as blocked
        \EndIf
        \EndFor

        \State \Call{UpdateSwitch}{} \Comment{Update lifetime and disconnect expired connections}
        \EndFor
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The dynamic experiment was conducted under the most restrictive conditions: an arrival rate equal to 1 (i.e., the switch is attempted to be fully loaded at each iteration), conflict of type 2, and the sorted routing strategy.
The blocking performance is evaluated using two metrics: the PI blocking, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-dynamic-exp}(a) to maintain consistency with the incremental experiment, and the PR, presented in Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-dynamic-exp}(b).
A key observation is that the dynamic experiment yields higher blocking rates than the incremental experiment.
This behavior arises because, when connections are removed, the sorted routing strategy becomes less effective than in the incremental case, where only new connections are added after each iteration.
Blocking results are presented as a function of the mean holding time of each port request, expressed in iterations.
For the PI blocking metric, the Double Dilated Benes architecture exhibits a maximum blocking rate between 2 and 3 iterations, reaching approximately 0.5 for the 128$\times$128 radix.
In contrast, the Benes and Dilated Benes architectures remain almost fully blocked across all radices and holding times.
The PR blocking metric is less restrictive, as it is evaluated on a per-request basis.
Under this metric, the Benes and Dilated Benes networks exhibit blocking probabilities in the ranges of 0.8-0.9 and 0.4-0.8, respectively, whereas the Double Dilated Benes architecture maintains a low blocking probability below 0.04 for the 128$\times$128 radix.
Overall, these results demonstrate that, under dynamic traffic conditions, the Double Dilated Benes architecture provides significantly better blocking performance than classical Benes-based networks.

\section{Architecture comparative analysis}\label{sec:architecture_comparison} % (fold)
Table~\ref{tab:ch4-summary-metrics} presents the overall comparison in terms of hardware complexity, optical performance, and blocking behavior for the networks considered.
To assess the integration density of photonic components, an additional metric based on the total number of crossings and the corresponding die size is introduced, assuming footprints of 250$\times$100 $um^{2}$ for the switching cell and 25$\times$25 $um^{2}$ for the crossing cell, which are typical values reported in the literature from Table~\ref{tab:ch4-sota-bb}.
Loss and SNR values correspond to the worst-case path and wavelength, using the component-level parameters reported in Table~\ref{tab:ch4-sota-bb}, while blocking is categorized as low, medium, or high relative to the other architectures.
As shown, Benes-like networks offer reduced hardware utilization—even a high-radix 128$\times$128 switch can be integrated within a 20$\times$20 $mm^{2}$ die—together with relatively low optical loss.
However, these architectures are RNB, and their blocking probability limits their applicability in scenarios where wide-sense or strictly non-blocking operation is required.
Notably, Double Dilated Benes improves the SNR to values above 45 dB and reduces blocking, while still maintaining a substantially lower component count compared to other architectures, which facilitates integration within constrained design areas.
The Dilated Banyan architecture exhibits excellent optical performance and SNB behavior, but at the cost of very large space utilization.
For a 128$\times$128 radix, the required die size reaches approximately 850 $mm^{2}$, exceeding the reticle limits of most standard silicon photonics foundries \cite{giewont300mmMonolithicSilicon2019}.
In contrast, the PILOSS network is wide-sense non-blocking (WSNB) but shows poor scalability beyond a 32$\times$32 radix in terms of optical loss, SNR, and integration capability, which remains inferior to that of Benes-like configurations.
However, it is worth noting that significant efforts have been made to improve the SNR performance of the PILOSS network by implementing switching elements based on dual Mach–Zehnder structures \cite{suzukiLargescaleSiliconPhotonics2025a}, suppressing first-order crosstalk without increasing network loss, at the expense of approximately doubling the number of MZIs.
The required SNR of a switching network naturally depends on the application.
Focusing on datacenter interconnects, transceivers are predominantly based on intensity modulation and direct detection (IMDD). For a typical PAM-4 format at 100Gbps per lane, an optical SNR (OSNR) above 30 dB is generally needed to achieve a BER below 1$\times$$10^{-4}$ \cite{shiTransmissionPerformanceComparison2017,zhangTransmissionSingleLane2016}.
Although this threshold does not directly apply to our reported SNRs, which quantify crosstalk rather than white noise, it provides a useful benchmark for the performance a network must achieve.

Based on these results, for low-radix switches (32 ports or fewer), the Dilated Banyan network emerges as the most suitable solution, as it is non-blocking and provides the best optical performance.
However, for higher radices (128 ports and beyond), the required chip area combined with current foundry constraints renders the Dilated Banyan architecture impractical today, only major advances in large-scale silicon photonic integration are expected to enable this architecture in the coming years, as fabrication technologies continue to mature.
Finally, Double Dilated Benes can be implemented with today’s silicon photonics technology and represent a practical path toward high-radix optical switching.
A key concept in making such networks viable is the notion of admissible blocking probability \cite{taniguchiOpticalCircuitSwitched2024a,okiDesignModelThreestage2025}, which defines the maximum tolerable likelihood of a connection request being blocked.
It is important to note that the dynamic blocking experiments considered here represent a highly conservative scenario: the network is assumed to be fully loaded at all times, and port usage is statistically independent.
In realistic deployments, such as AI datacenter networks, traffic patterns tend to be more predictable and often scheduled \cite{wangTopoopTCooptimizingNetwork,liaoMixNetRuntimeReconfigurable2025,addanki2025vermilion}, although not fully synchronous~\cite{8535157}.
Therefore, the acceptable level of blocking depends on the application requirements.
This analysis assumes worst-case traffic scenarios, and thus the observed blocking rates can be viewed as an upper bound or a worst-case estimate.
In practice, scheduling can mitigate collision, making moderate blocking probabilities tolerable~\cite{weintraub2025packetloss, lin2018deep}, indicating that RNB networks could still be practical in datacenter applications.
Moreover, when blocking events occur due to shared switching cells, first-order crosstalk may be accepted to reduce blocking by sacrificing SNR. Likewise, depending on the application, rearranging could be applied to stablish the new connection while preserving second-order order crosstalk conditions.
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:ch4_conclusion}
To conclude, we have presented a graph-based modelling methodology to analyze and benchmark application-specific silicon photonic switch architectures, addressing the scalability challenges toward high-radix optical circuit switching.
We demonstrated feasible architectures capable of meeting the radix and performance requirements demanded by modern networking applications, while remaining compatible with current manufacturing capabilities.

However, when deployed in a datacenter network, the radix of a single optical circuit switch is insufficient to satisfy the network connectivity requirements.
Therefore, multiple switching devices must be interconnected.
In this scenario, the coordination and management of multiple interconnected switches introduce complexity that cannot be addressed at the device level alone.
Software-Defined Networking (SDN) becomes essentially within this context, enabling centralized control, dynamic reconfiguration, and efficient orchestration across the network of optical circuit switches.
In the next chapter addresses this need by introducing a gNMI-based controller for silicon photonic switches, providing the standardized, model-driven management interface required to integrate these devices within an software defined network.
