\section{Programmable integrated photonics} \label{sec:pip}
% from eic to pic
Since the emergence in the mid-20th century, electronic integrated circuits (EICs) have played an important role in technological and economic development~\cite{sedra2011microelectronic}.
In recent decades, photonic integrated circuits (PICs) have attracted growing interest in both academia and industry as a complementary technology, offering new opportunities to address some fundamental limitations of EICs.
Specifically, PICs can offer lower power in high speed data transmission, as optical signals avoid resistive losses inherent to electrical interconnects.
In addition, PICs provide higher bandwidth, because optical signals are not fundamentally limited by electrical resistor–capacitor (RC) time constants, allowing much higher modulation speeds in waveguide transmission~\cite{tian2025photonic, 81373}.

% give the programmability to integrated photonic
Similar to the evolution of electronic systems toward programmable and software-defined architectures~\cite{oldfield1995field}, programmable integrated photonics (PIPs) is a new paradigm in which the photonic circuit can be defined and controlled through software~\cite{capmany2020programmable, bogaerts2020programmable}.
PIP leverages a single integrated photonic hardware to implement multiple functionalities or reconfigure circuit behaviors by applying external control signals.
% operation and application
As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:ch1-pip-overview}(a), PIP is built upon reconfigurable photonic elements such as Mach-Zehnder interferometers (MZIs), microring resonators, or crossing array.
By applying external control signals to these building block units, the amplitude and phase of optical signals can be independently adjusted.
A typical PIP processor overview is demonstrated in Figure~\ref{fig:ch1-pip-overview}(b), consisting of optical fan-in and fan-out stages for coupling light into and out of the chip, together with an electronic control interface for driving the photonic actuators.
In addition, optional interfaces for high-speed radiofrequency (RF) signals utilizing modulators and photodetectors.
The key of PIP lies in its reconfigurable photonic core, which can generally be categorized into feedforward and recirculating architectures according to the signal propagation, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:ch1-pip-overview}(c).
Feedforward mesh correspond to a directed, without loop propagation of signals through cascaded interferometric units, where light travels from inputs to outputs without forming closed paths.
Such architectures are particularly suitable for deterministic linear transformations and matrix vector multiplications, making them well suited for optical computing and neuromorphic photonics systems.
Moreover, the unidirectional propagation inherent in feedforward designs also facilitates high speed signal routing, making these architectures attractive for applications such as optical interconnects and switching.
In contrast, recirculating propagation arises when feedback loops are introduced into the mesh, allowing optical signals to circulate within resonant structures~\cite{reconfig}.
These loops naturally support frequency selective behavior, which are essential for implementing optical filters, delay lines, and resonant sensing functionalities.
Furthermore, through appropriate configuration, recirculating meshes can also synthesize feedforward circuits~\cite{perez2017multipurpose}, although the additional propagation paths and feedback loops generally introduce higher insertion loss and crosstalk.
Consequently, these recirculating architectures are commonly regarded as general-purpose photonic meshes, and have also been termed multipurpose meshes in the literature.

% software programmability
The flexibility of PIP enables the reconfiguration of optical paths and synthesis of diverse circuits within the same physical footprint.
On top of the hardware reconfigurability, PIP systems can be further abstracted through mathematical modeling, where photonic circuits are represented using frameworks such as linear transformations or graph-based methodology~\cite{lopez2020auto, chen2020graph, rausell2025programming}.
This enables high level programming of photonic functionalities, shifting the design paradigm from device level tuning to application driven programming.
In addition, algorithmic optimization methods can be introduced to improve the efficiency, accuracy, and robustness of circuit synthesis and control~\cite{perez2020principles}.

% importance for networking
PIP offers a reconfigurable and high bandwidth photonic platform, and its importance is particularly significant in emerging interconnect scenarios where modern large scale computing systems and data movement across nodes have become a dominant bottleneck in both performance and energy efficiency.

\section{Networking interconnects in AI infrastructure} \label{sec:background}
% background: development of AI
Over the past decade, the rapid rise of artificial intelligence (AI) has propelled computing models from simple neural networks to architectures containing trillions of parameters~\cite{Lu02012019, tu2024overview}.
Since the introduction of the Transformer architecture in 2017 \cite{transformer}, the computational demand for training large models has grown exponentially, doubling approximately every six months, which is far beyond Moore's Law \cite{9891914, 7878935}.
This gap between the slowing pace of Moore's Law transistor scaling and the rapidly increasing computational demands of AI has driven a shift beyond single chip optimization to large-scale distributed computing \cite{distributedcomputing}.
As a result, the computing paradigm is no longer confined to a single device or system boundary.
% How AI reshaped interconnect
Modern data center (DC) infrastructures are increasingly organized as hierarchical systems, with on-chip and network level communication mechanisms increasingly adopting similar paradigms for scalability and performance \cite{noc, jerger2022chip, kas2012toward}.
At the lowest level, intra package integration and chiplet links such as Nvidia's NVLink-C2C enable high bandwidth, low latency cache coherent communication among CPUs, GPUs, and high-bandwidth memory (HBM), facilitating tightly integrated heterogeneous computing systems \cite{10767590, chiplet, nvc2c}.
At the server level, multiple XPUs (e.g., GPUs or TPUs) within a single chassis or cabinet are interconnected using high speed scale up fabrics, enabling tightly coupled computing with high bandwidth and low latency.
Examples include NVIDIA's NVLink, the UALink provided by AMD and the Scale-Up Ethernet (SUE) interconnect that developed by Broadcom \cite{nvidia_nvlink, ualink_consortium, velaga2025sue}.
Beyond the server boundary, large numbers of computing nodes are connected across racks within a data center using high performance networking technologies such as InfiniBand or Ethernet, enabling distributed scale-out workloads \cite{pfister2001introduction, aramide2024ultra}.
At the largest scale, hyperscale clusters coordinate tens of thousands of accelerators to operate as a single synchronized system for large model training and other data intensive applications \cite{kokolis2025revisiting, broadcom_scale_across_ai}.
As communication distance increases from micrometers to kilometers, interconnect media and architectures evolve to meet the corresponding bandwidth, latency, and energy requirements.
% How AI reshape the traffic
Meanwhile, traffic patterns within network have been gradually evolving.
Traditional workloads were dominated by north-south traffic between external clients and servers.
In contrast, modern AI workloads generate east-west traffic, consisting of intensive server-to-server communication within the network \cite{Sbriglia2024AIDataCenter, nooruzzaman2021hyperscale}.
Such traffic is typically high bandwidth and latency sensitive, and often follows structured and predictable communication patterns across many computing nodes \cite{wangTopoopTCooptimizingNetwork, avin2025revolutionizing}.
These characteristics place significantly different demands on the network compared to traditional burst and independent flows.

% EPS as current datacenter solution
Most data center networks have been predominantly built upon electronic packet switching (EPS), originally optimized for north-south traffic \cite{1455410}.
Each packet is handled independently, and transmitted hop-by-hop across leaf-spine topologies.
Although transmission uses optical fiber, each switch still performs optical-electrical-optical (O-E-O) conversions, where incoming optical signals are converted to electrical form, buffered and processed, and then retransmitted optically.
This architecture offers flexibility and compatibility with existing networking protocols.
% EPS problem
However, as bandwidth and scale continue to increase, and as traffic patterns gradually evolve to east-west communication, EPS faces growing limitations.
Frequent O-E-O conversions raise power consumption, especially with high speed serializer/deserializer (SerDes) circuits required for 400G to 1.6T links \cite{keysight_800g_16t}.
In parallel, electronic switch Application-Specific Integrated Circuits (ASICs) also encounter physical I/O constraints, and while techniques such as co-packaged optics (CPO) improve interface density, they do not remove the power burden of electronic switching cores \cite{minkenberg2021co}.
In addition, the store-and-forward model introduces buffering and queuing delays, leading to variable latency \cite{1455410}.
For tightly synchronized large scale AI training, even small network delays can reduce overall system efficiency.

\section{Toward optical circuit switching} \label{sec:toc}
% Motivates OCS
These limitations indicate that purely electronic packet switching may not scale efficiently for future AI infrastructures.
As mentioned earlier, traffic during AI training involves large, structured, and persistent data exchanges among fixed node groups.
Processing every packet individually therefore introduces unnecessary overhead.
This motivates Optical Circuit Switching (OCS) \cite{macnica_coherent}.
% OCS introduction
OCS establishes dedicated physical layer interconnects between endpoints instead of routing packets hop by hop.
Optical signals are directly routed from N input to N output ports through free space optics or integrated photonic circuits, forming transparent paths without intermediate electronic processing.
Because switching occurs entirely in the optical domain, OCS is protocol and rate transparent.
The same fabric can support higher data rates without redesigning switch ASICs.
In addition, once a circuit is configured, data remains in the optical domain, avoiding repeated O-E-O conversion and significantly reducing steady-state power consumption.

% OCS application in datacenter
OCS is already emerging in practical systems across both scale-out and scale-up domains (Figure~\ref{fig:ch1-ocs-apps}).
At the scale-out level, Google's Apollo system uses MEMS based (Micro-Electro-Mechanical Systems) OCS as a reconfigurable spine, enabling flexible topology adaptation and hardware upgrades \cite{urata2023apollo, jupiter}.
NVIDIA has also explored OCS for failure resilience and dynamic network rewiring \cite{patronas2024optical}.
In scale-up architectures, OCS supports hardware disaggregation by connecting compute nodes to shared memory or storage pools through dedicated optical circuits, improving resource utilization \cite{8293994}.
It has further been investigated for high bandwidth GPU interconnects beyond the limits of electrical links, extending direct, traffic-aware connections across larger physical domains \cite{addanki2025vermilion}.

% silicon photonic based programmable integrated photonic ocs
Most current OCS deployments are based on free-space optical switching technologies, including MEMS mirrors, piezo-electric beam steering systems, and liquid-crystal-on-silicon (LCoS) devices \cite{lumentum_ocs, polatis7000, coherent_ocs}.
These technologies generally offer low insertion losses (typically around 0.5-1.5 dB) and support large port counts, but they are relatively bulky and typically operate on millisecond timescales, limiting responsiveness to dynamic AI workloads \cite{1244924, CignalAI2025OCS4Q25}.

Silicon photonics (SiPh) has emerged as a promising alternative by enabling the monolithic integration of photonic components on a single chip, benefiting from the substantial investment made in CMOS(Complementary Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor) fabrication infrastructure and process technologies.
By leveraging the maturity and scalability of the semiconductor manufacturing ecosystem, SiPh platforms achieve high integration density, improved manufacturing yield, and reduced cost per port compared with discrete optical implementations.
Practical SiPh switching systems typically incorporate polarization management techniques, such as polarization controllers or polarization diversity architectures~\cite{Shahwar2024Polarization}, since most silicon photonic components are optimized for transverse-electric (TE) polarization.
In addition, semiconductor optical amplifiers (SOAs) could be integrated or co-packaged with SiPh switching fabrics to compensate for accumulated insertion losses and maintain sufficient optical link budgets in large scale switching systems~\cite{7328999}.

Beyond their high integration density, SiPh switching fabrics also provide significant performance advantages.
Depending on the tuning mechanism, such as thermo-optic or electro-optic modulation, they typically achieve reconfiguration times ranging from microseconds to sub-milliseconds \cite{bogaerts2020programmable}.
Unlike free-space optical switches, SiPh devices contain no mechanically moving parts, resulting in improved robustness, reduced wear out mechanisms, and enhanced long term operational stability.
These characteristics make SiPh particularly well suited for optical circuit switching in environments requiring frequent and rapid network reconfiguration.

As discussed in the previous section~\ref{sec:pip}, the reconfigurability offered by PIP naturally extends the advantages of SiPh to optical circuit switching.
Through software defined optical path configuration, a single PIP chip can realize multiple switching matrices and dynamically adapt network connectivity based on traffic demands without hardware modifications.
Such flexibility is particularly attractive for AI data center networks, where communication patterns vary across different training workloads and collective communication phases~\cite{si2025collective}.
The maturity of programmable SiPh technology is further evidenced by the early stage commercial optical switching solutions from companies such as iPronics, Salience Labs, and nEye Systems~\cite{cignal2025startuptracker}.
Taken together, these characteristics make PIP a promising platform for scalable optical circuit switching in AI driven communication infrastructures.

\section{Challenges} \label{sec:challenges}
Despite the advantages of silicon photonics for optical circuit switching, deployment of large scale SiPh based switching fabric in hyperscale AI networks still faces complexity that has not yet been fully addressed.

% software defined control mechanism for programmable photonic circuit.
AI training workloads follow several traffic patterns between computing nodes, which requires the precise coordination of thousands of integrated actuators to real time reconfiguration of photonic circuit processor \cite{bogaerts2020programmable}.
Performing such on chip reconfiguration in PIP through the traditional one-by-one sequential tuning of actuators can introduce significant delay, making it operationally impractical for large switching fabrics and potentially disrupting cluster scheduling and training efficiency.
In this regard, auto-routing algorithms have been explored to develop point-to-point optical interconnects in a programmable photonic circuit, with fault tolerant and self-healing capabilities but applied in a small scale circuit of a few actuators \cite{lopez2020auto}.
More recently, a novel N$\times$N automated switching algorithm has also been reported theoretically, but experimental validation is still lacking \cite{vanden2023automated}.
Therefore, there remains room to improve interconnect algorithms in PIP, particularly in efficiently identifying paths in large scale circuits and in supporting more complex communication patterns, while their effectiveness on hardware remains to be validated.

% hardware scalability problem.
Although small scale SiPh switches have been proven, scaling these systems to meet industrial demands introduces fundamental challenges that necessitate new research \cite{tanizawa2015ultra, suzukiUltracompact882014, tanizawa2016ultra, suzuki2025band}.
The high radix scaling and architecture design present significant difficulties.
Supporting modern clusters may require switches with 128$\times$128, 256$\times$256 or even higher ports, involving tens of thousands of integrated actuators on a single chip \cite{patronas2024optical, perez2025large}.
At this density, cumulative insertion loss and crosstalk become substantial, and conventional circuit topologies are insufficient, designing these systems demands a careful balance between port count, signal integrity, physical footprint, etc,.

% system level integration.
Moreover, a major gap exists in system level Software-Defined Networking (SDN) integration.
Currently, datacenter networking stacks are primarily optimized for electronic packet switching.
While standardized management interfaces exist for some Layer-1 optical components (such as ROADMs) \cite{7503119}, the unified, software-defined control of Layer-1 optical resources in network environments remains less mature than electronic switching infrastructures and higher layer network management frameworks.
This represents a broad and complex challenge in integrating optical circuit switches into modern data centers.
Within this context, it is important to focus on approaches that allow OCS to be more easily interfaced with SDN frameworks, addressing part of this integration gap.

\section{Objectives} \label{sec:objective}
Considering the critical role of programmable integrated photonics in scaling AI data center interconnects, this thesis develops a comprehensive, software-defined platform for large-scale integrated photonic switching.
Rather than treating circuit design, control, and system integration as isolated problems, this work establishes a coupled, cross-layer methodology that links physical photonic hardware with abstract programming models and network-level control, as summarized in Fig.~\ref{fig:ch1-thesis}.

\begin{figure}[h]
      \begin{center}
            \includegraphics{figures/ch1-thesis.pdf}
      \end{center}
      \caption{Cross layer structure for software-defined integrated photonic switching.
            This thesis addresses three key aspects of the stack:
            (a) graph based PIP hardware control.
            (b) scalable PIP switching design.
            (c) SDN-enabled system level control.
      }\label{fig:ch1-thesis}
\end{figure}

To achieve this, the thesis defines and implements a vertically integrated technological stack that enables software-defined circuit level control and systematic investigation of hardware design through a unified graph-based representation.
Furthermore, a system level control framework is developed that enables silicon photonic optical circuit switches to operate within a software-defined network.
Specifically, the contributions are organized across the following interconnected layers:
\begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Circuit abstraction and synthesis:} A graph based model is introduced to represent recirculating programmable integrated photonic circuits, enabling translation of high level connectivity requirements into physical device configurations.
            Based on this abstraction, automated circuits synthesis algorithms are developed to support software-defined generation of photonic circuits for networking applications, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ch1-thesis}(a).

      \item \textbf{Architecture design methodology:} Based on graph theory abstraction, different switching architectures are modelled through distinct connection schemes between building blocks, resulting in varying hardware complexity.
            Routing algorithms are developed on the corresponding switch circuit graphs to characterize blocking behavior, while signal integrity analysis is performed using defined S-parameters model to evaluate optical performance.
            These metrics are jointly used to assess the scalability and performance of the architectures, giving practical guidelines for large-scale SiPh optical circuit switch design, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ch1-thesis}(b).

      \item \textbf{System level control and integration:} A software-defined network based control framework is implemented to enable dynamic configuration of photonic circuits.
            Leveraging a gNMI-based interface, the proposed system integrates optical circuit switch into software-defined network environments, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ch1-thesis}(c).
\end{itemize}
Overall, this work provides a unified approach that links hardware level design, programmable control, and network level integration, enabling scalable and manageable photonic switching systems.

\section{Structure of the thesis} \label{sec:structure}
The thesis is organized to offer a comprehensive account of the theoretical background, methodological developments, and experimental validation associated with the research contributions.
The thesis is structured into the following chapters:

\begin{itemize}
      \item Chapter 1: \nameref{chap:intro} This chapter provides the research background and motivation, focusing on the transition from traditional electronic switching to integrated photonic switching.
            It reviews the architecture of modern AI data center networks and highlights the increasing demand for scalable silicon-photonic optical circuit switching.
            The chapter then defines the research objectives and outlines the main contributions of the thesis, providing an overview of the structure of the subsequent chapters.
      \item Chapter 2: \nameref{chap:fundamentals} This chapter presents the theoretical foundations for PIP circuits.
            It introduces key integrated photonic components and circuit topologies, then demonstrates a graph-theory methodology for circuit modeling and routing, which enables automated control and programming at the photonic layer.
            It also outlines SDN principles to support the design of an SDN based control layer for integrating optical circuit switching into networks.
      \item Chapter 3: \nameref{chap:applications_on_pip} This chapter demonstrates optical networking applications implemented on hexagonal programmable photonic processors.
            By applying graph-theory-based routing algorithms, the work replaces manual and sequential tuning procedures with automated programming methods.
            The chapter presents implementations of optical interconnects, multicasting, and circuit switching functions on a single processor, followed by a systematic evaluation of performance metrics.
      \item Chapter 4: \nameref{chap:ocs_archs} This chapter uses graph theory to model high-radix silicon photonic switch architectures, including Benes-like, PILOSS, and Dilated Banyan topologies.
            Different architectures are constructed through their graph connectivity and analyzed in terms of hardware complexity, blocking behavior, and signal integrity.
            Routing algorithms are applied to the corresponding switch graphs to evaluate blocking probability, while optical performance is assessed through insertion loss and signal-to-noise ratio.
            The resulting comparisons provide design guidelines for scalable silicon photonic optical circuit switches.
      \item Chapter 5: \nameref{chap:sdn_for_ocs} This chapter presents the experimental demonstration of a silicon photonic switch managed via a gNMI-controlled SDN framework.
            It introduces a custom YANG model designed to abstract the switching resources and connection configurations of the OCS device, enabling standardized and programmable control.
            The chapter validates the operational flow, showcasing lossless performance through integrated SOA compensation and exceptionally low crosstalk,
            establishing a standardized pathway for integrating OCS into AI infrastructures.
      \item Chapter 6: \nameref{chap:discussion_and_conclusions} This chapter examines the design, modeling, and evaluation of large-scale silicon photonic optical circuit switches in data center networks.
            It discusses key physical layer challenges, including optical insertion loss and crosstalk, and analyzes their impact on scalability and performance.
            Practical deployment considerations, such as integration with existing network architectures and software-defined control, are also addressed.
            Finally, the chapter outlines future directions toward ultra-large-radix switching fabrics, including hardware architectural and application level approaches to extend system scalability and capacity.
\end{itemize}

At the end of the thesis, a section is provided with a complete list of the author's merits and contributions to scientific journals and conferences.
