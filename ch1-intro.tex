\section{Interconnects in network infrastructure} \label{sec:background}
% background: development of AI
Over the past decade, the rapid rise of artificial intelligence (AI) has driven computing systems from relatively simple neural networks toward models containing trillions of parameters \cite{Lu02012019, tu2024overview}.
Since the introduction of the Transformer architecture in 2017 \cite{transformer}, the computational demand for training large models has grown exponentially, doubling approximately every six months—far beyond Moore's Law \cite{9891914, 7878935}.
This gap between the slowing pace of Moore’s Law transistor scaling and the rapidly increasing computational demands of AI has driven a shift beyond single-chip optimization to large-scale distributed computing \cite{distributedcomputing}.
As a result, computing paradigm is no longer confined to a single device or system boundary.
% How AI reshaped interconnect
Modern data centers (DCs) infrastructures are increasingly organized as hierarchical systems, with on-chip and network-level communication mechanisms increasingly adopt similar paradigms for scalability and performance \cite{noc, jerger2022chip, kas2012toward}.
At the lowest level, intra-package integration enables communication among chiplets, processor cores, and high-bandwidth memory through advanced technologies such as silicon interposers. \cite{10767590, chiplet}
At the server level, multiple accelerators within a single chassis are interconnected using high-bandwidth links such as NVLink to support tightly coupled scale-up computing \cite{nvidia_nvlink, song2025survey}.
Beyond the server boundary, large numbers of nodes are connected across racks within a data center using high-performance networking technologies such as InfiniBand or Ethernet, enabling distributed scale-out workloads \cite{pfister2001introduction}.
At the largest scale, hyperscale clusters coordinate tens of thousands of accelerators to operate as a single synchronized system for large-model training and other data-intensive applications \cite{kokolis2025revisiting}.
As communication distance increases from micrometers to kilometers, interconnect media and architectures change to meet bandwidth, latency, and energy constraints.
In contemporary systems, the energy cost associated with data movement often exceeds the cost of computation itself, making interconnect design a central architectural challenge \cite{MWINUKA2026100927, en19030722}.

% EPS as current datacenter solution
Most data center networks have been predominantly built upon electronic packet switching (EPS).
Although transmission uses optical fiber, each switch still performs optical-electrical-optical (O-E-O) conversions.
Incoming optical signals are converted to electrical form, buffered and processed, and then retransmitted optically.
This architecture offers flexibility and compatibility with existing protocols.
% EPS problem
However, as bandwidth and scale continue to increase, EPS faces growing limitations.
Frequent O-E-O conversions raise power consumption, especially with high-speed serializer/deserializer (SerDes) circuits required for 400G to 1.6T links \cite{keysight_800g_16t}.
In parallel, electronic switch ASICs (Application-Specific Integrated Circuits) also encounter physical I/O constraints, and while techniques such as co-packaged optics improve interface density, they do not remove the power burden of electronic switching cores \cite{minkenberg2021co}.
In addition, the store-and-forward model introduces buffering and queuing delays, leading to variable latency \cite{1455410}.
For tightly synchronized large-scale AI training, even small network delays can reduce overall system efficiency.

\section{Toward optical circuit switching} \label{sec:toc}
% Motivates OCS
These limitations indicate that purely electronic packet switching may not scale efficiently for future AI infrastructures.
Unlike traditional internet traffic, AI training involves large, structured, and persistent data exchanges among fixed node groups \cite{wang2023topoopt, avin2025revolutionizing}.
Processing every packet individually therefore introduces unnecessary overhead.
This motivates Optical Circuit Switching (OCS) \cite{macnica_coherent}.
% OCS introduction
OCS establishes dedicated physical-layer connections between endpoints instead of routing packets hop by hop.
Optical signals are directly routed from input to output ports through free-space optics or integrated photonic circuits, forming transparent paths without intermediate electronic processing.
Because switching occurs entirely in the optical domain, OCS is protocol and rate transparent.
A deployed fabric can support higher data rates without redesigning switch ASICs.
In addition, once a circuit is configured, data remains optical, avoiding repeated O-E-O conversion and significantly reducing steady-state power consumption.

% OCS application in datacenter
OCS is already emerging in practical systems across both scale-out and scale-up domains (Figure~\ref{fig:ch1-ocs-apps}).
At the scale-out level, Google's Apollo system uses MEMS-based (Micro-Electro-Mechanical Systems) OCS as a reconfigurable spine, enabling flexible topology adaptation and hardware upgrades.
NVIDIA has also explored OCS for failure resilience and dynamic network rewiring.
In scale-up architectures, OCS supports hardware disaggregation by connecting compute nodes to shared memory or storage pools through dedicated optical circuits, improving resource utilization.
It has further been investigated for high-bandwidth GPU interconnects beyond the limits of electrical links, extending direct, traffic-aware connections across larger physical domains.

% integrated photonic ocs
Most current deployments rely on MEMS mirrors to steer light.
While reliable and low-loss, MEMS switches are relatively bulky and operate on millisecond timescales, limiting responsiveness to dynamic AI workloads.
Silicon photonics (SiPh) addresses these issues by integrating optical components such as Mach-Zehnder interferometers or microring resonators onto CMOS-compatible (Complementary Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor) chips.
These solid-state devices enable microsecond-scale reconfiguration, higher integration density, and co-packaged deployment with GPUs or CPUs, reducing footprint and electrical loss.
Benefiting from wafer-scale fabrication and the absence of mechanical moving parts, SiPh offers improved scalability, robustness, and suitability for next-generation AI data centers.

\section{Challenges} \label{sec:challenges}
Despite the advantages of silicon photonics for optical circuit switching, deploying large-scale SiPh-based switches in hyperscale AI data centers still faces complexity that has not yet been fully addresses.
While small-scale switches have been proven, scaling these systems to meet industrial demands introduces fundamental challenges that necessitate new research.
First, high-radix scaling and architecture design present significant difficulties. Supporting modern clusters may require switches with 128×128, 256×256 or even higher ports, involving tens of thousands of integrated actuators on a single chip.
At this density, cumulative insertion loss and crosstalk become substantial, and conventional circuit topologies are insufficient, designing these systems demands a careful balance between port count, signal integrity, physical footprint, etc,.
The most critical operational bottleneck, however, is the large-scale control and configuration of these actuators.
In a high-radix switch matrix, establishing a data path or reconfiguring the entire network fabric requires the precise coordination of thousands of integrated actuators.
Although AI training traffic is typically predictable, switching between different communication patterns or workload configurations still requires reconfiguring the optical switch fabric.
Performing such reconfiguration through the traditional one-by-one sequential tuning of actuators can introduce significant delay, making it operationally impractical for large switching fabrics and potentially disrupting cluster scheduling and training efficiency.
Without a software-defined tool to instantly map logical interconnect requests into hardware states, the potential for microsecond switching remains locked behind the inability to manage the hardware's complexity in real-time.
Moreover, a major gap exists in system-level SDN integration.
Currently, AI networking stacks are primarily optimized for electronic packet switching.
While standardized management interfaces exist for some Layer-1 optical components (such as ROADMs), the unified, software-defined control of Layer-1 optical resources in AI data center environments remains less mature than electronic switching infrastructures and higher-layer network management frameworks.
This represents a broad and complex challenge in integrating optical circuit switches into modern data centers.
Within this context, it is important to focus on approaches that allow Layer-1 optical resources to be more easily interfaced with SDN frameworks, addressing part of this integration gap.

\section{Objectives} \label{sec:objective}
Considering the critical role of programmable photonic integrated circuits (PICs) in scaling AI data center interconnects, this thesis focuses on the development of a comprehensive, software-defined ecosystem for large-scale integrated photonic switching.
The primary aim is to establish a vertically integrated technological stack that covers physical-layer circuit synthesis, architectural design, and system-level control, thereby enabling programmable photonic processors to be seamlessly integrated into modern AI infrastructures.
Specifically, this thesis aims to develop a scalable and manageable silicon photonic OCS solution for data center interconnects.
This project begins with the development of a graph-based programming methodology to automate the synthesis and mapping of photonic circuits, bridging the gap between high-level logic and physical actuator configurations.
It then investigates scalable photonic switching architectures, aiming to resolve the trade-offs between hardware complexity and signal integrity.
This includes conducting in-depth blocking analysis and physical-layer simulations to evaluate insertion loss (IL) and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) in data center environments.
Finally, an integrated gNMI-based control system is implemented for Layer-1 SiPh OCS, enabling standardized, software-defined reconfiguration and management within AI data center networks.

\section{Structure of the thesis} \label{sec:structure}
The thesis is organized to offer a comprehensive account of the theoretical background, methodological developments, and experimental validation associated with the research contributions.
The remainder of this document is structured into the following chapters:

\begin{itemize}
      \item Chapter 1: \nameref{chap:intro} This chapter provides the research background and motivation, focusing on the transition from traditional electronic interconnects to integrated photonic switching.
            It reviews the architecture of modern AI data centers networks and highlights the increasing demand for scalable silicon-photonic optical circuit switching.
            The chapter then defines the research objectives and outlines the main contributions of the thesis, providing an overview of the structure of the subsequent chapters.
      \item Chapter 2: \nameref{chap:fundamentals} This chapter presents the theoretical foundations for photonic integrated circuits.
            It introduces key photonic components and circuit topologies, then demonstrates a graph-theory-based framework for circuit modeling and routing, which enables automated control and programming at the photonic layer.
            It also outlines Software-Defined Networking (SDN) principles to support the design of an SDN-based control layer for integrating optical circuit switching into data center networks.
      \item Chapter 3: \nameref{chap:applications_on_pip} This chapter demonstrates optical networking applications implemented on hexagonal programmable photonic processors.
            By applying graph-theory-based routing algorithms, the work replaces manual and sequential tuning procedures with automated programming methods.
            The chapter presents implementations of optical interconnects, multicasting and circuit switching functions, followed by a systematic evaluation of performance metrics.
      \item Chapter 4: \nameref{chap:ocs_archs} This chapter develops a graph-theory-based modeling and benchmarking approach for high-radix silicon photonic switch architectures, including Benes-like, PILOSS and Dilated banyan topologies.
            It analyzes hardware complexity and optical performance by evaluating metrics such as component count, insertion loss, and signal-to-noise ratio.
            Blocking probability under different routing strategies is also investigated.
            The chapter provides design guidelines for scalable and efficient optical switching fabrics suitable for data center applications.
      \item Chapter 5: \nameref{chap:sdn_for_ocs} This chapter presents the experimental demonstration of a silicon photonic switch managed via a gNMI-controlled in SDN.
            It introduces a custom YANG model designed to minimize control overhead while optimizing hardware performance across diverse connection types.
            The chapter validates the operational flow, showcasing lossless performance through integrated SOA compensation and exceptionally low crosstalk,
            establishing a standardized pathway for integrating OCS into AI infrastructures.
      \item Chapter 6: \nameref{chap:discussion_and_conclusions} The final chapter synthesizes the research findings, discussing critical challenges such as scalability, optical losses, and the maturity of reconfigurable topologies for industrial use cases.
            It addresses the ongoing efforts toward SDN standardization and the current gaps in AI network optimization software. Finally, the chapter outlines the necessary developments for the commercialization and large-scale deployment of optical circuit switching systems.
\end{itemize}

At the end of the thesis, a section is provided with a complete list of the Author's merits and contributions to scientific journals and conferences.
