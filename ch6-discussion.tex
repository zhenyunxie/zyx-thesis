\chapter{Discussion and conclusions}\label{chap:discussion_and_conclusions} % (fold)
This chapter builds on the previous analysis of the design, modeling, and evaluation of large-scale silicon photonic optical circuit switches, and discusses them in the broader context of data center networking.

It first explores the impact of optical insertion loss and crosstalk on large-radix photonic switching fabrics.
It then addresses practical challenges in integrating optical circuit switching into existing data center infrastructures.
Finally, it outlines potential future research directions, such as scalable multi-chip switching fabrics and failure-resilient circuit mechanisms, and summarizes the main conclusions of the thesis.

\section{Discussion} \label{section:discussion}
\subsection{Optical insertion losses} \label{subsec:insertion_loss}
Optical insertion loss is one of the most critical factors limiting the scalability of silicon photonic interconnects,
as the accumulated loss introduced by switching elements, waveguide crossings, and other photonic components increases significantly with the radix of the switching fabric,
thereby directly affecting the feasibility of large-scale deployments in data center environments.

In Chapter~\ref{chap:applications_on_pip}, programmable photonic processors based on hexagonal meshes were investigated as a flexible platform for implementing optical switching functions.
While such programmable architectures provide strong versatility, they inherently suffer from limited scalability when applied to high-radix switching fabrics.
As a result, Chapter~\ref{chap:ocs_archs} presents several application-specific switching architectures and further analyzed the insertion loss as a function of the two dominant contributors: switching cells and waveguide crossings.
Figure~\ref{fig:ch6_pic_loss_projection} presents this projection for 128- and 256-port radices, where the grey dashed lines indicate state-of-the-art building blocks, representing the current technological baseline.
As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ch6_pic_loss_projection}a, for the 128×128 case, all architectures except PILOSS can achieve a total loss below 5 dB provided that the switching-cell and crossing losses are reduced to 0.15 dB and 1.5 mdB, respectively.
For even higher radices, such as 256×256 in Figure~\ref{fig:ch6_pic_loss_projection}b, this 5 dB PIC-loss target remains feasible as long as the switching-cell and crossing losses are reduced below 0.1 dB and 1 mdB, respectively.
It should be noted that the loss calculations also include additional building blocks listed in Table~\ref{tab:ch4-sota-bb}, which are required to realize the complete circuit and significantly contribute to the overall PIC performance.
These components account for on-chip polarization management, coupling efficiency, and propagation loss.

One of the important challenge arises from polarization management.
Silicon waveguides are inherently polarization sensitive and typically support only a single polarization mode with low loss.
However, optical signals transmitted through optical fibers generally arrive with arbitrary polarization states.
To address this issue, many integrated photonic systems adopt a polarization-diversity scheme, in which the incoming signal is split into two orthogonal polarization components that are processed independently through duplicated switching fabric.
Although this approach ensures polarization-independent operation, it effectively doubles the required switching resources, thereby increasing chip area, integration complexity, and causes the overall optical loss.

The coupling efficiency of photonic interfaces is influenced not only by the design of edge couplers but also by packaging and assembly processes.
Even the improvements in edge couplers by using advanced silicon-nitride with losses of 0.1–0.2 dB can further reduce the total PIC loss,
fiber-array attachment and flip-chip integration can introduce mechanical stress and chip warpage, which may affect optical alignment to reduce the coupling efficiency.
In large photonic integrated circuits, even small mechanical deformations can lead to misalignment between optical interfaces.
For example, in a 32×32 switch, the maximum center deflection caused by packaging-induced warpage is typically limited to approximately 20~µm.
As the the switching radix getting higher, these tolerances become even more stringent, requiring careful co-design of the photonic layout and packaging process.

A potential approach to mitigate the accumulated losses in large-scale switching fabrics is through integrate optical amplification system.
Semiconductor optical amplifiers (SOAs) are suitable candidates for this purpose, as data center interconnects typically operate in the O-band, where SOAs can provide effective optical gain.
However, introducing active components also brings additional system-level considerations, as optical amplifiers may introduce noise and signal distortions that affect transmission quality.
Therefore, performance metrics such as signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), bit-error ratio (BER), and transmitter dispersion eye closure (TDECQ), need to be carefully evaluated to ensure reliable operation.
